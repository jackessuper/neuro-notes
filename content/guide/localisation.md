!!! note "tl;dr:"
    **Longitude = which pathway. Latitude = which level.** Find where they cross and you've found the lesion.<br>
    Two questions do most of the work: *upper or lower motor neuron?* and *what else is affected?*

Localisation can seem daunting but is mostly a map-reading exercise. It's a very logical area of medicine, which can be very satisfying (especially in light of a lot of the other uncertainties in neurology, medicine, and life. Localisation is a happy place).

Three long pathways run the length of the nervous system (the meridians of longitude). Crossing them are the levels: cortex, brainstem, cord segments, roots, plexus, nerve, junction, muscle (the parallels of latitude).

The pattern of weakness and sensory loss tells you *which pathways*. Everything else on the examination tells you *which level*.

The symptoms and signs tell you **where** the problem is. They almost never tell you **what** it is. That's what [tempo and context](differential.html) are for.

## Step 0: Upper or lower motor neuron? 🔀

Often the first step: This will tell you central vs peripheral

| | Upper motor neuron | Lower motor neuron |
|---|---|---|
| Weakness | "Pyramidal" pattern: arm extensors and abductors, leg flexors and dorsiflexors | In the territory of a nerve, root or muscle |
| Tone | Increased | Decreased |
| Reflexes | Brisk | Reduced or absent |
| Plantar | Up-going | Down-going |
| Wasting | Little or late | Early and marked |

!!! danger "The pathology is at the level of the lower motor neuron signs"
    Upper motor neuron signs mean the lesion is *above* that level, somewhere in the central nervous system. The classic miss: a patient who can't walk gets a lumbar spine MRI, which only shows the cauda equina (L3 to S1 roots). If the legs are spastic with brisk reflexes and up-going plantars, the problem is above L1 (where the cord ends) and the scan has looked in the wrong place.

## The meridians: three pathways 🧭

1. **Motor (corticospinal).** Motor cortex (precentral gyrus) → corona radiata → internal capsule → cerebral peduncle → ventral pons and medulla → **crosses at the foramen magnum** → lateral column of the cord → anterior horn cell → root → plexus → nerve → neuromuscular junction → muscle.
2. **Vibration and proprioception (dorsal columns).** Receptor → nerve → plexus → dorsal root → *ipsilateral* dorsal column → **crosses at the foramen magnum** → medial lemniscus → thalamus → parietal cortex.
3. **Pain and temperature (spinothalamic).** Receptor → nerve → root → **crosses within a segment or two of entering the cord** → anterolateral cord → lateral brainstem → thalamus → cortex.

!!! tip "The crossing rule"
    Motor and vibration/proprioception cross at the top of the cord. Pain and temperature cross at the bottom, where they enter. So:

    - Left-sided UMN signs or vibration loss: lesion on the **left below** the foramen magnum, or the **right above** it.
    - Left-sided pain and temperature loss: lesion on the **right**, anywhere from the cord up.
    - Face, arm and leg all on the same side: lesion above the mid-pons.
    - Face on one side, body on the other (a "crossed" deficit): brainstem.

Three immediate consequences:

- **Weakness** puts the lesion somewhere between the motor cortex and the muscle.
- **Sensory symptoms** put it somewhere between the skin and the parietal cortex.
- **Both together** rule out muscle, neuromuscular junction, anterior horn cell and motor root, which carry no sensory fibres.

## The parallels: which level? 📏

Now add everything that isn't weakness or sensory loss. Each extra finding is a parallel of latitude.

| Weakness plus… | Level |
|---|---|
| Marked wasting | Peripheral nervous system (central lesions don't waste much) |
| A cranial nerve palsy | Brainstem, at the level of that nerve |
| Visual field loss, dysphasia, neglect, cortical sensory loss | Cortex |
| Both legs, and a sensory level on the trunk | Spinal cord, at that level |
| Sensory loss in one nerve or one root territory | Peripheral nerve or root |

### Cortex and hemisphere

Cortical signs localise instantly: a non-fluent dysphasia is the dominant frontal lobe, a homonymous hemianopia is behind the chiasm on the opposite side, neglect and cortical sensory loss (graphaesthesia, two-point discrimination, sensory inattention) are parietal.

### Brainstem

The cranial nerves are floor numbers:

- **Midbrain:** 3, 4
- **Pons:** 5, 6, 7, 8
- **Medulla:** 9, 10, 11, 12

A cranial nerve palsy puts the lesion in that nerve or in the brainstem at that level. Right arm and leg UMN signs with a *left* 3rd nerve palsy is a left midbrain lesion (Weber's syndrome). Nerves 3, 4, 6 and 12 (the factors of 12) leave close to the midline; the rest leave laterally, which helps separate medial from lateral brainstem syndromes.

!!! tip "The rule of 4"
    The brainstem has 4 lateral structures, all beginning with S: Spinothalamic, spinocerebellar, sympathetic (causing a Horner's syndrome if affected), and the sensory nucleus for CN 5. There are 4 midline structures, all beginning with M: Motor (corticospinal), medial lemniscus (vibration + proprioception), medial longitudinal fasciculus/MLF (causes an INO), motor nuclei of III, IV, VI and XII

!!! warning "False localising sign"
    A 6th nerve palsy (and occasionally a 3rd) can be caused by raised pressure and downward herniation rather than a lesion at the nerve.

### Spinal cord

Bilateral UMN signs in the legs mean cord (or, less often, bilateral brain or brainstem). The level comes from a **sensory level** on the trunk, a **reflex** that's lost at the lesion and brisk below it, or a **myotome** that's weak in an LMN pattern at the level and UMN below it.

!!! note "Worked example: a woman who can't walk"
    A 70-year-old with months of stiff, weak legs, unsteady in the dark, and a feeling of tight stockings. Her legs are weak and hypertonic, and reflexes are increased. She has reduced sensation up to her umbilicus.

    Both legs weak with UMN signs: Central cord or bilateral pyramidal lesions. Vibration loss means the dorsal columns are involved, and sharp touch loss means spinothalamic. The UMN signs = UMN lesion above L1, but the sensory loss at the umbilicus localises to ~T10 in the cord.

!!! warning "Roll the patient over"
    Dermatomes sit higher on the back than on the front. A sensory level at exactly the same height front and back is not structural, and suspicious for functional sensory loss.

### Root, plexus and nerve

If you establish there is LMN involvement it is good to try and localise further. Generally speaking: LMN lesions can localise to the root, the plexus, or the nerve. Identifying which involves learning the pattern of motor, sensory and reflex involvement of each.

The easiest thing to do is to first learn the actions of peripheral nerves, then roots. If it doesn't fit with either of those then consider a plexus lesion. More about this later (peripheral neuroanatomy is a few chapters in and of itself!)

## Dermatome landmarks 🗺️

Nobody remembers the whole map. Remember the pegs and interpolate:

| Landmark | Root |
|---|---|
| Middle finger | C7 (C6 is the thumb side, C8 the little-finger side) |
| Clavicle, where C4 meets T2 | T2 |
| Xiphisternum | T8 |
| Umbilicus | T10 |
| Groin | T12 / L1 |
| Knee | L3 crosses it |
| Outer shin and dorsum of the foot | L5 |
| Outside of the foot and the sole | S1 |

On the face, the trigeminal nerve stops about two-thirds of the way back over the scalp (the greater occipital nerve, C2, takes over) and in front of the ear. The **angle of the jaw** is C2/3, not trigeminal: sensory loss that spares the angle of the jaw is a 5th nerve lesion; loss that includes it is a spinothalamic (brainstem) lesion. Upper lip is V2, lower lip V3, and the corneal reflex is V1.

## Putting it together 🧩

Say it in this order and the listener can follow: *the pathways involved, the side, the UMN/LMN fork, then the parallel of latitude that pins the level.*

!!! note "Worked example: a weak right hand"
    A 35-year-old with weakness confined to the right hand, wasting between the thumb and index finger, and pins and needles in the little finger, the medial half of the ring finger, and the medial palm and dorsum to the wrist. Examination: reduced pinprick in that distribution, weak abduction of the fingers, weak flexion of the fingertips of the 4th and 5th but not the 2nd and 3rd; thumb normal.

    Weakness: motor pathway. Sensory loss: nerve, root or spinothalamic. Wasting: peripheral. The weakness pattern (interossei, FDP to 4 and 5, FDP to 2 and 3 spared) is the ulnar nerve at or above the elbow, and the sensory map is ulnar, not C8. **Ulnar neuropathy at the elbow.**

!!! tip "On the ward round"
    "UMN-pattern weakness of the right arm and leg with right lower facial weakness: left corticospinal tract above the mid-pons. With the expressive dysphasia, that's left frontal cortex."
