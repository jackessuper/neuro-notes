!!! note "The whole chapter in two lines"
    **Tempo + Localisation = Syndrome**<br>
    **Syndrome + Context = Differential diagnosis**

Neurology is terrible because one complaint ("I can't walk properly") can come from a dozen different places, and a dozen different diseases. This is however the reason that neurology is actually great. You've just gotta lean in and break things down: Stop jumping from complaint to diagnosis and break things down into the *syndrome* instead. Once you start with a syndrome and move out everything starts to make sense, and it stops feeling like you're just doing the same 3 tests on every patient (although don't worry! You'll get to do that sometimes too).

## Why bother? 🤔

Doing it this way means you:

- boil any presentation down to its core, so even rare things become findable
- order fewer scans, and fewer wrong scans
- stop chasing your tail interpreting lots of tests that are slightly abnormal
- know how much examination this particular patient actually needs
- keep a running list of the bits that don't fit, so you can come back to them when the illness evolves

Doing this dance is a skill that is you can work on for a whole career, and is one of the reasons that neurology is so good. Trust me.

## Step 1: Tempo ⏱️

Tempo is how the symptoms **started and evolved**. It is defined by the onset (not by how long the patient has had the problem): a fixed deficit from a stroke three years ago is still a hyperacute tempo.

| Tempo | Onset | Think of | Classic examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hyperacute | Seconds to minutes | Vascular, seizure, some headaches, syncope, trauma, toxic/metabolic | Stroke, tonic-clonic seizure, cluster headache, overdose |
| Acute | Minutes to hours to days | Infection, inflammation, toxic/metabolic, structural | Bacterial meningitis, optic neuritis, uraemia, extradural haematoma |
| Subacute | Days to weeks to months | Infection, inflammation, neoplasm, toxic/metabolic, structural | TB, autoimmune encephalitis, glioblastoma, B12 deficiency, subdural |
| Chronic | Months to years | Genetic, neurodegenerative, neoplasm, toxic/metabolic, structural | Huntington's, motor neuron disease, meningioma, lead, cervical stenosis |

The categories blur into each other a bit. What matters is you can tease it out when needed:

- **Hyperacute:** the patient or family can tell you exactly where they were and what they were doing. There is a sharp line between normal and not.
- **Acute:** the baseline is still clear, but there is no exact minute. "He had a fever last night, a headache this morning, and by this evening he wasn't making sense."
- **Subacute:** the edge is blurry. Anchor it to events: "Were you like this at your daughter's birthday?" Patients often remember the first time it *mattered* (the trip on the kerb) rather than when it started.
- **Chronic:** there may be no start at all, only a time in the past when the family is sure things were fine.

!!! warning "Tempo traps"
    The tempo of the *symptoms* is not always the tempo of the *disease*. A tumour can grown for years and then suddenly cause a (hyperacute) seizure. Venous disease and compressive lesions are notorious for presenting at any tempo. For episodic illnesses (seizures, relapsing MS), define the tempo of each attack *and* of the overall course: a first-ever seizure that becomes several a day over two weeks is a subacute illness, and that points you at infection or inflammation.

## Step 2: Localisation 📍

Localisation is working out **where** the lesion is. Most of your localisation comes from history, not examination. We'll go into this in more detail in the next chapter so don't stress. You'll be glad to know that there's whole textbooks written on this stuff, and again you can choose to spend a whole career refining this.

In a nut-shell though: Work from the top down and drill as deep as you can:

1. **Central or peripheral?**
2. **Central:** cerebral hemisphere (cortex, subcortical white matter, basal ganglia, thalamus), brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla), cerebellum, or spinal cord (which tracts, which level)?
3. **Peripheral:** root, dorsal root ganglion, plexus, nerve (axon or myelin), neuromuscular junction (pre- or post-synaptic), or muscle (proximal or distal)?

The [localisation chapter](localisation.html) has the patterns.

!!! warning "One lesion doesn't always explain everything"
    Some patients have multifocal disease, and some have more than one disease. Occam's razor (one cause for everything) gets weaker with age and Hickam's dictum (patients can have as many diseases as they please) gets stronger. An 85-year-old with osteoarthritis, an old stroke and diabetes who walks badly may have no single unifying lesion. A healthy 12-year-old who walks badly almost certainly does.

## Step 3: Tempo + localisation = syndrome 🧠

The syndrome is your assessment: "an acute, rapidly progressive cervical myelopathy", or "a chronic length-dependent sensorimotor neuropathy". That phrase, not a disease name, is what you should be able to say at the end of the bedside bit.

As a trainee, do tempo first. It is always available from the history, whereas localisation needs the examination too and is the harder skill. Experienced neurologists often go localisation first. Both roads end at the same syndrome.

## Step 4: Syndrome + context = differential 🗂️

Context is the rest of the history: past medical and surgical history, drugs, family, social, the notes. It is not something you *reason* your way to; it is just there. Context orders the differential but rarely removes anything from it. Hyperacute loss of consciousness in a child means very different things if she hit her head half an hour ago versus being found next to an empty pill bottle.

!!! danger "Context is also where anchoring lives"
    Context is the fastest way to narrow a differential and the commonest reason for getting it wrong. Define the syndrome **before** you let the context in.

## The four kinds of "diagnosis" 🏷️

Part of what makes neurology confusing is that one symptom has four layers of answer. Take a 50-year-old man who comes to ED with difficulty walking.

- **Anatomical diagnosis:** where is it? Run down the examination: inattention or neglect (parietal), a visual field defect (chiasm), weakness from anywhere between cortex and muscle, an extrapyramidal gait, loss of proprioception, cerebellar ataxia. A simple complaint, a long list.
- **Syndromic diagnosis:** add the associated symptoms and the tempo. "Hyperacute right leg weakness" is a syndrome, and it has a short differential.
- **Aetiological diagnosis:** the mechanism. Ischaemic stroke.
- **Pathological diagnosis:** the root cause. Cardioembolism from atrial fibrillation.

Only the last two tell you what to do, but you only get there reliably by going through the first two. Modern imaging makes it tempting to leapfrog straight from complaint to scan. That road leads to the wrong tests, delays, and diagnoses built on incidental findings. It also stops you learning.

## When not to be clever 🚑

The syndromic approach is for patients who give you time. When someone is critically ill, treat the reversible things first and work the syndrome out later: the unconscious patient gets naloxone whether or not anyone admits to opiates. In the emergency setting, keep two columns in your head, **most likely** and **can't miss**, and rule out the can't-miss column even when it doesn't fit the tempo and localisation story.

Remember too that subacute and chronic illnesses may not have declared their full syndrome when you first meet them. Sometimes you have to act before the picture is complete, and that is fine as long as you know that is what you are doing.

!!! tip "On the ward round"
    Present it in this order: tempo, localisation, syndrome, context, differential, then the tests you want. Consultants visibly relax when they hear it.
