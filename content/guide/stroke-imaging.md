!!! note "tl;dr:"
    **Non-contrast CT, in order: 1. Blood? 2. Hyperdense vessel? 3. Early ischaemic change (ASPECTS)? 4. Anything else?**<br>
    **CTA: is there an occlusion someone can pull out, and what in the neck caused it?**

The CT is an extension of your examination. You have already localised the deficit at the bedside, so you know where to look before the first slice loads. Read it the same way every time, in the same order, while the patient is still on the table. A normal-looking CT in the first hours of a big stroke is common and expected: the scan is there to exclude blood and find the clot, not to prove the stroke.

This chapter is the quick read. A slower, picture-by-picture run through CT, CTA and CT perfusion is coming in a later section.

## Before you start 🧭

- **Density.** CSF is black, fat is dark, brain is grey, acute blood is bright, bone and calcium are brighter still. Lesions on CT are *hypodense* or *hyperdense* (on MRI they are hypo- or hyper*intense*).
- **Normal bright things.** Calcified choroid plexus, pineal, basal ganglia and falx are white and sharp-edged. Blood is bright but never quite as white as bone.
- **Window it.** Narrow the window to pick up subtle grey-white loss (a "stroke window" of roughly width 40, level 40, or whatever your PACS calls it). The default brain window hides early ischaemia.
- **Compare sides.** Nearly every early sign is an asymmetry. Scroll the same level on both hemispheres before you call anything.
- **Scroll bottom to top**, from the foramen magnum to the vertex, and look at the posterior fossa properly: it is where the artefact lives and where the basilar hides.

## Step 1: Is there blood? 🩸

Look for anything hyperdense that is not bone or calcium. Acute blood is bright; it fades to isodense over a week or two and ends up hypodense, so a subacute subdural can be nearly invisible against the brain.

| Where | What it looks like | Think |
|---|---|---|
| Deep: basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum | Rounded hyperdensity in the deep grey or brainstem | Hypertensive small-vessel bleed |
| Lobar: near the cortex | Hyperdensity in a lobe, often with surrounding oedema | Amyloid angiopathy in the older patient; in the young, look for an underlying lesion (AVM, tumour, venous thrombosis) |
| Subarachnoid | Bright sulci and basal cisterns | Aneurysm (or trauma) |
| Subdural | Crescent along the inner skull, crosses sutures | Trauma, anticoagulants, the elderly |
| Extradural | Lens shape, stops at sutures | Trauma, skull fracture |

If you find an intracerebral haemorrhage, describe six things: **location, volume, mass effect and midline shift, intraventricular extension, hydrocephalus, and anything suggesting a secondary cause.**

!!! tip "Volume in ten seconds: ABC/2"
    A = largest diameter on the axial slice (cm), B = the diameter perpendicular to it (cm), C = number of slices it is visible on × slice thickness (cm). Volume ≈ A × B × C / 2 in mL. Over 30 mL is a bad sign and one of the points on the ICH score.

!!! danger "Blood means no thrombolysis"
    Any acute haemorrhage is an absolute contraindication to lysis. It is not a reason to skip the CTA: the angiogram looks for an aneurysm, AVM or venous thrombosis underneath, and a **spot sign** (a dot of contrast inside the haematoma) predicts that it will grow.

Two traps. A bleed inside a wedge of hypodensity in an arterial territory is probably **haemorrhagic transformation** of an infarct, not a primary bleed. And a dense dural sinus beside a lobar haemorrhage is **venous sinus thrombosis** until the CT venogram says otherwise.

## Step 2: Is there a hyperdense vessel? 🧵

A clot that is rich in red cells is denser than flowing blood, so you can sometimes see the occlusion on the plain scan.

- **Hyperdense MCA sign:** a bright M1 running laterally from the carotid terminus, compared with the other side.
- **Sylvian dot sign:** a bright dot in the Sylvian fissure, a distal M2 clot.
- **Hyperdense basilar:** look for it on purpose, in front of the pons, in every patient with a posterior circulation story. It is routinely missed because of beam-hardening artefact at the skull base.
- **Dense sinus or cortical vein:** the venous version. Hyperdensity in the superior sagittal or transverse sinus, or a cord-like cortical vein, means venous thrombosis.

Absence means nothing: many large vessel occlusions are fibrin-rich and invisible on plain CT. Recent contrast (a CTA or angiogram earlier in the day) makes every vessel bright. The CTA is the answer either way.

## Step 3: Early ischaemic change and ASPECTS 📉

Ischaemic brain swells before it dies. The early signs are all about **loss of grey-white differentiation** and **swelling**:

1. Obscured lentiform nucleus (the basal ganglia go first: the lenticulostriate arteries are end arteries with no collaterals).
2. Loss of the insular ribbon.
3. Blurred cortical grey-white margin.
4. Sulcal effacement.
5. Frank hypodensity, which means established infarct.

Swelling alone can reverse with reperfusion, so early sulcal effacement is not by itself a reason to withhold treatment. Clear hypodensity across a whole territory is tissue that has already gone; if it is "too big and too dark" for the time the patient is reporting, check the clock again.

!!! note "ASPECTS in one table"
    Start at 10 and take one off for each of ten MCA regions showing early ischaemic change. Two slices, five regions each:

    | Ganglionic slice (level of the basal ganglia and thalamus) | Supraganglionic slice (level of the lateral ventricle bodies) |
    |---|---|
    | **C** caudate | **M4** anterior MCA cortex |
    | **L** lentiform | **M5** lateral MCA cortex |
    | **IC** internal capsule | **M6** posterior MCA cortex |
    | **I** insula | |
    | **M1, M2, M3** anterior, lateral, posterior MCA cortex | |

    10 is a normal scan. The lower the score the bigger the established core. The main thrombectomy trials took ASPECTS ≥ 6; the newer large-core trials show benefit down to 3 to 5, so a low score is a conversation with the stroke consultant, not a closed door. ASPECTS only applies to the MCA and ICA territory.

!!! warning "Posterior circulation"
    ASPECTS does not apply. Look at the cerebellar hemispheres, the pons, the thalami and the occipital lobes for hypodensity, and at the fourth ventricle for effacement. A swollen cerebellum pressing on the fourth ventricle is a neurosurgical conversation as well as a stroke one.

## Step 4: Anything else? 🔍

The last pass is for the mimic you would be embarrassed to miss: a tumour with oedema, bilateral subdurals, an abscess, a giant aneurysm, hydrocephalus, or old infarcts and small-vessel change that explain a chronic story rather than today's deficit. Ask whether what you see **explains the deficit you examined**. A right hemiparesis with a tiny old left lacune on CT is still an acute stroke until proven otherwise.

## CTA: finding the clot 🔎

The angiogram is done in the same sitting, aortic arch to vertex. Intracranial-only ("circle of Willis") misses the neck; neck-only misses the brain. Look at the thin source images as well as the MIPs: axial MIPs show M1 well, coronal MIPs show the ICA terminus and basilar.

Trace each vessel from the neck upward and ask three questions:

1. **Is there an occlusion?** A vessel that stops, or a filling defect. The ones that matter for thrombectomy are the **ICA terminus, M1, proximal M2 and the basilar**; ACA, PCA and vertebral occlusions are described but retrieved less often.
2. **Is it tandem?** An intracranial occlusion with a neck ICA occlusion or tight stenosis upstream changes the plan.
3. **How are the collaterals?** Compare pial vessel filling with the other side (easier on multiphase CTA). Good collaterals buy time; poor collaterals mean the core grows fast.

!!! tip "Trace the basilar the whole way"
    Follow both vertebrals through the foramen magnum to where they join, then up the basilar to the top. Tapering to a cut-off is an occlusion. A short filling defect in the mid-basilar is easy to scroll past.

A normal CTA in a patient with a clear deficit usually means a **lacunar** stroke (small-vessel occlusion, nothing to retrieve) or a mimic. If venous thrombosis is on your list, ask for the venous phase or a CT venogram: the **empty delta sign** is a filling defect in the sinus with contrast around it.

## CTA: the neck 🦒

The neck answers "why", and it drives secondary prevention, so do not stop at the skull base.

| Finding | Clues | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Carotid stenosis** | Calcified plaque within 1 to 1.5 cm of the bifurcation | Symptomatic stenosis of 50 to 99% is an endarterectomy within two weeks: early recurrence risk is high |
| **Dissection** | Starts 2 to 3 cm beyond the bulb (or vertebral V3), tapered "flame" occlusion, long irregular "string sign", intimal flap, pseudoaneurysm. No calcium. Young patient, neck pain, Horner's | Not a contraindication to lysis; access for thrombectomy is harder |
| **Vertebral or arch atheroma** | Irregular plaque | Medical management, antiplatelet and risk factors |
| **Carotid web** | Shelf-like filling defect at the bulb | A cause of stroke in the young; easy to miss |

## CT perfusion in one paragraph 🌈

CTP is a colour map of blood flow. **Core** (dead) is the tissue with cerebral blood flow below about 30% of normal; **penumbra** (ischaemic but salvageable) is tissue with a delay (Tmax over 6 seconds) but preserved blood volume. The automated software gives you a core volume, a penumbra volume and a mismatch ratio, and a ratio above 1.8 with a small core is the shape of a patient who benefits from reperfusion. It earns its keep in the 6 to 24 hour and unknown-onset windows, is unreliable for lacunes and the posterior fossa, and can look like a stroke after a seizure or with a tight carotid. Do not decide on the colours without looking at the plain CT and the patient.

## Contrast worries 💧

- **Kidneys:** "neurons over nephrons". The risk of contrast nephropathy is low even with renal disease, and waiting for a creatinine costs brain. Scan, then give fluids.
- **Allergy:** hives in the past can be covered with steroid and antihistamine around the scan. True anaphylaxis means MR angiography or ultrasound instead.

## Saying it out loud 🎤

Report it in the order you read it, then say what it means.

!!! note "Worked example: a left MCA syndrome"
    68-year-old with sudden right face and arm weakness and dysphasia, last well 90 minutes ago, NIHSS 14.

    "Non-contrast CT: no haemorrhage. Hyperdense left M1. Loss of grey-white differentiation in the left lentiform and insula, ASPECTS 8. No other lesion. CTA arch to vertex: left M1 occlusion over about a centimetre, no tandem lesion, collaterals fair. Neck: no stenosis or dissection. So: a left M1 occlusion with a small early core. Lyse now and call the thrombectomy team."

!!! note "Worked example: the one you nearly miss"
    74-year-old found drowsy with dysarthria and a gaze palsy, 'probably a seizure'.

    "Non-contrast CT: no haemorrhage. Hyperdense basilar artery. No cerebellar or pontine hypodensity yet. CTA: the basilar fills from the vertebrals then tapers to a cut-off in the mid segment. Basilar occlusion. Thrombectomy call now."
